# Neural odometry for Cardboard on low-end Android

**Assessment date:** 2026-10-07

**Status:** feasibility review; no experimental pose model is enabled in the app.

## Recommendation

Do not ship inertial-only neural odometry as the headset's room-position tracker yet. Keep the current honest split:

- **ARCore available:** metric 6DoF room tracking in the Full edition.
- **ARCore unavailable:** 3DoF head rotation, using Android's game-rotation-vector sensor and the existing neck model. Do not synthesize room position by double-integrating the accelerometer.

For a future no-Google-services experiment, a **small neural inertial model used as a low-rate, uncertainty-weighted aid to visual tracking** is more plausible than an IMU-only model controlling room-locked VR. RNIN-VIO is a useful research reference for that hybrid idea, but its public artifacts are not a drop-in Android tracker and its reported phone is not evidence for low-tier performance.

## Why NIO alone is a poor VR position source

A gyroscope/accelerometer can provide fast rotation and short-term motion cues, but it does not observe a fixed point in the room. Neural inertial odometry (NIO) replaces some inertial integration with a learned motion prior; it does not add an external position anchor. Its predictions are therefore tied to training motion and accumulate relative-position error. That is especially risky for Cardboard: head motion is usually small, arbitrary, and often stationary—not the continuous walking that dominates smartphone pedestrian-odometry datasets. A small bias that might be tolerable for coarse pedestrian dead reckoning can make a supposedly stationary VR room visibly drift.

TinyOdom is an encouraging example of hardware-aware, compact temporal-convolutional NIO, but its paper reports **2.5–12 m error over 60 seconds across its application setups** while targeting ultra-resource-constrained processors. That is valuable for embedded navigation research, not a room-scale pose guarantee for VR. Smaller inference cost does not solve the accuracy/generalization requirement.

## Candidate comparison

| Approach | Why it is attractive | Main blocker for NextVR/Cardboard |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure NIO** (RoNIN/TLIO-style; TinyOdom for tiny targets) | Uses only phone sensors; avoids camera power and camera-lens calibration; a small TCN can be inexpensive. | No room observation or loop closure. Existing models learn pedestrian/device-motion priors, not the small head-mounted movements we need. Position drift and uncertainty must be measured on our own headset data. |
| **Neural VIO** | Can use visual information to correct inertial drift, while a learned model can supply a motion prior or remain useful when image features are weak. | Still needs time-aligned camera/IMU input, a scale/geometry solution, and robust tracking. A neural model does not automatically remove sensor alignment or camera-model assumptions. Vision inference also competes with the camera, hand tracking, rendering, and battery budget. |
| **Neural-aided visual-inertial filter** (RNIN-VIO-style) | Best research direction of the three: the network provides a relative-motion estimate and uncertainty; a filter combines it with camera features and high-rate IMU propagation. | Significant integration and data-collection work. RNIN-VIO's public repository describes itself as the inertial neural network code, not a complete Android application. Its paper's Android demo used a Huawei Mate 20 Pro, 30 Hz 512×384 images and 200 Hz IMU; the neural inertial module ran at about 2 Hz. This does not establish that it meets low-tier phone power, latency, or accuracy needs. |

RoNIN's benchmark contains natural human motion and varied phone placement, and TLIO demonstrates uncertainty-aware neural displacement updates fused in a filter. Those are useful ideas, but neither dataset description is a substitute for recording phones fixed in a Cardboard headset and validating the motion we actually need.

## Calibration reality

If “no calibration” is a hard requirement, pure NIO avoids camera intrinsics and camera-to-IMU extrinsics—but gives up visual correction and cannot promise stable room position. A hybrid visual-inertial system still has to associate image measurements with the correct sensor axes and timestamps. Learning may make those assumptions more tolerant; it does not make the sensor relationship disappear.

Android exposes camera intrinsic-calibration metadata through Camera2 on devices that provide it, and ARCore exposes per-session image intrinsics and camera poses. Availability and correctness of the Camera2 metadata vary by device, which is why a custom camera-based tracker should not silently assume factory calibration exists. ARCore is the supported path already doing that platform-specific work for the app.

## Practical experiment if no-GMS 6DoF becomes a priority

1. **Collect headset-mounted data first.** Record timestamped accelerometer and gyroscope samples, camera frames, device/model identifiers, and tracking dropouts across several budget phones. Include stationary head sway, small translations, quick turns, low light, blur, and camera occlusion—not only walking with a handheld phone. Use ARCore or external motion capture as ground truth where possible.
2. **Prototype the learned part off the render thread.** Start with a compact causal TCN that predicts short-window relative displacement **and covariance**. Keep rotation and pose propagation at sensor/display rate; let a separate filter consume the slower learned updates. Treat low confidence as “translation unavailable,” not as a plausible-looking pose.
3. **Use vision as the correction, not a large image network as the whole tracker.** If avoiding Google services is essential, first benchmark a small conventional feature front end on low-resolution frames, then test whether the learned inertial factor improves loss recovery enough to justify its CPU and battery cost. A fully learned VIO network should be a later comparison, not the first implementation.
4. **Set a minimum-phone go/no-go gate before exposing a mode.** Measure stationary drift, translation error on repeatable head motions, relocalization jumps, end-to-end pose latency, CPU use, thermal throttling, and battery draw. Test CPU-only and any device accelerator separately; NNAPI/NPU support is not uniform. Until the prototype meets predeclared room-stability and latency limits on the slowest supported phone, keep it experimental and keep the user in 3DoF.

The app already includes LiteRT for other features, so model execution infrastructure exists. That is not the missing part: the missing pieces are headset-specific training data, a sensor-fusion implementation, trustworthy confidence handling, and low-end-device validation.
